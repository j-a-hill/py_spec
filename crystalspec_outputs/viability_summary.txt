IS A DTNB-PROBED DIFFRACTION / SPECTROSCOPY EXPERIMENT VIABLE?

Short answer: yes at low dose rate, no at full beam, and the limit is the
protein's own radiation chemistry rather than the spectrometer.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE THREE THINGS THAT HAD TO BE TRUE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. THE LABEL SIGNAL IS REAL AND X-RAY DRIVEN            [figure V1]

   The TNB label absorbs at 412 nm.  Under irradiation that absorbance falls,
   in every condition, in DTNB-soaked films but not in matched unlabelled
   controls.  A DTNB-soaked film with the shutter CLOSED, processed
   identically, changes by -0.0016 over the same 20 s interval -- inside the
   0.004 baseline noise.  Nothing happens without X-rays.

   One caveat, stated because it would otherwise be found by a reader: that
   control is flat over 20 s but NOT over its full 100 s record, where it
   drifts to -0.027 (peak-to-peak 0.028, about 7x the noise floor and
   comparable to the label loss).  The cause is not identified -- it may be
   lamp or detector drift, or slow sample change.  The comparison is therefore
   made at a matched time inside the analysed window rather than at each
   trace's own last frame, which is also why the bar chart in figure V1 is
   read at 2.9 s: that is the longest time EVERY condition still has data for,
   the 100 % transmission film having failed optically by 3 s.

   At a matched 2.9 s the four irradiated conditions have lost 0.017 to 0.028
   absorbance units against a baseline noise of 0.004.
   That is a factor of 4 to 7 above the floor.

2. IT IS DETECTABLE INSIDE A CRYSTAL'S LIFETIME         [figure V2]

   Figure V2 plots the quantity that matters directly: the SIGNED label
   signal against dose (panel a) -- it falls, because the label is lost, and
   is plotted falling rather than as |dA| so the direction on the page
   matches the chemistry.  A dashed threshold line marks the detection level
   (the noise floor), each curve's own detection dose is marked where it
   crosses that line, and each curve is labelled with the total change it
   reaches by 0.4 MGy.  Panel b compares that detection dose against the
   published room-temperature diffraction lifetime range, 0.10 to 0.37 MGy
   (Owen et al. 2012): three of four conditions (5, 25, 50 % T) detect at 0.07-0.90 of the range
   of published lifetimes; the fourth (100 % T) detects at 0.65-2.50 of that
   range, i.e. it needs MORE dose than the crystal survives for four of the
   six published lifetimes, and less for only two.

   No half-life or single decay constant is fitted for this comparison.  A
   fitted decay constant on these traces is window-dependent -- it changes
   1.4 to 2.5x depending on how much of the curve the fit is given, because
   the decay is not a single exponential (see the note on kinetics below).
   Reading the dose axis directly, against a fixed noise floor, avoids that
   problem entirely: no exponential is fitted to make this comparison.

   transmission   dose rate     detection dose   fraction of crystal lifetime
      5 %        0.052 MGy/s    0.025 MGy      0.07 - 0.25   VIABLE
     25 %        0.260 MGy/s    0.058 MGy      0.16 - 0.60   VIABLE
     50 %        0.521 MGy/s    0.088 MGy      0.23 - 0.90   VIABLE
    100 %        1.042 MGy/s    0.243 MGy      0.65 - 2.50   needs averaging

   Below 1 the label change is measurable before the crystal loses its
   diffraction.  Three of four transmission levels are under 1 across every
   published lifetime.  Only full beam is not, and there the fix is averaging
   over several crystals rather than a different technique.

   The trend is the useful part, and panel b of figure V2 plots it directly:
   SLOWER delivery is better.  Detection dose rises 9.7-fold from 5 % to
   100 % transmission (0.025 to 0.243 MGy) for a 20-fold increase in dose
   rate, because faster irradiation destroys the label before enough spectra
   accumulate.

   The six lifetimes used above are all from Owen et al. 2012
   (doi:10.1107/s0907444912012553, "Outrunning free radicals in
   room-temperature macromolecular crystallography"), for named systems at
   stated rep rates: BEV2 at 1 Hz 0.097 MGy, A2AAR at 1 Hz 0.108, FcgRIIIa
   stop-start 0.186, A2AAR at 25 Hz 0.192, FcgRIIIa continuous below 11 Hz
   0.257, FcgRIIIa at 25 Hz 0.373.  They are a bracket from comparable
   systems, not measurements on HGD, which is why the comparison is reported
   as a range rather than a single ratio.

3. THE CONTRAST LIMIT IS UNDERSTOOD                     [figure V3]

   The protein itself develops a strong absorption band near 310 nm under
   irradiation, in BOTH arms.  It is 2.1 to 2.9 times larger
   than the label loss, and it overlies 328 nm where the intact
   protein-S-S-TNB adduct absorbs.

   Two consequences for experiment design:
     * The intact adduct cannot be followed.  Its band is buried.
     * The label must be read at 412 nm AS A DIFFERENCE against a matched
       unlabelled control, because the 310 nm band's red tail reaches into
       the 412 nm window and partially cancels the loss.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT THIS SUPPORTS SAYING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  "Loss of a DTNB label from an HGD crystal is spectroscopically detectable
   at 412 nm within the dose a room-temperature crystal survives, provided
   the dose is delivered slowly.  At 0.052 MGy/s the change clears the
   measured noise floor by 0.025 MGy, 0.07 to 0.25 of the published
   diffraction lifetime.  A joint diffraction / spectroscopy measurement is
   therefore feasible in principle, but it must be fast, it must be read as a
   difference against an unlabelled control, and the dominant spectral change
   is the protein's own radiation chemistry rather than the label."

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT THIS DOES NOT SUPPORT SAYING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  * No concentration or occupancy.  These are films of randomly oriented
    crystals plus mother liquor; the crystalline volume fraction is unknown
    and varies severalfold between positions.  Only relative changes mean
    anything.
  * No mechanism.  Whether the label is destroyed in place or released and
    diffuses out of the probed volume cannot be distinguished here.
  * No rate constant.  A fitted decay constant on these traces grows 1.4 to
    2.5 times depending on the dose window chosen, so the decay is not a
    single exponential and any single quoted rate would be an artefact of the
    window.
  * Nothing about the RT multicrystal geometry as a spectroscopy platform.
    These are thin films, not the single large crystals online spectroscopy
    would need.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------


--------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHY NOT A HALF-LIFE?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

Half-life and the fitted decay constant d1 are the same quantity in different
units (t_half = ln(2) * d1), so the reason one cannot be quoted is the reason
the other cannot either: d1 is not stable against how much of the curve is
used to fit it.  Truncating the DTNB-25 difference trace at 0.6, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0
and 3.0 MGy gives d1 = 0.25, 0.38, 0.61, 0.85 and 1.11 MGy -- a 4.4-fold
spread for the same underlying data, because the decay has a fast initial
component and a slower one that only shows up once enough dose has been
given.  The full-range fit (4.68 MGy) is larger again.  A single half-life
quoted from any one of these would misrepresent how fast the change actually
happens at low dose, which is the regime a real experiment would run in.

A two-component (biexponential) fit was tried as well, since a fast-plus-slow
decay is what the window-dependence above implies.  Fitted over the full
dose range it does fit better (R2 0.92-1.00 against 0.59-0.95 for one
exponential -- the single exponential does particularly badly on DTNB_50,
R2 0.59), but the two rate constants it returns are LESS stable across
windows than the single rate was: for DTNB_25, the fast time constant is
0.044 MGy fitted to a 1.0 MGy window, 0.112 MGy fitted to a 2.0 MGy window,
and 0.994 MGy fitted to the full 25 MGy record -- a 22-fold spread across
just three window choices.  For the two highest dose rates (50 % and 100 %
T), the fit already pins at least one of the two rates to its bound by the
2.0 MGy window and at the full range; DTNB_50 at a 1.0 MGy window is the one
case that does NOT hit a bound.  Two free rates are not more identifiable
from these traces than one; they are less.  Neither is reported as a rate
constant for that reason.

A THIRD attempt, tried in response to a direct request: fit ONLY the fast
part of a two-component decay, on the reasoning that it's the physiologically
relevant one anyway.  This looked promising at first -- the fast time
constant from a biexponential fit held roughly stable within each condition
(DTNB_5 0.069-0.074, DTNB_25 0.038-0.040, DTNB_50 0.091-0.100 MGy) across
small windows from 0.5 to 0.8 MGy, in contrast to the
single exponential's d1, which never stabilised.  But the apparent stability
is an artefact of fit degeneracy, not a real fast component: the SLOW time
constant (d2) is pinned to its own lower bound (0.50 MGy, the smallest value
the fit was allowed) at every one of those "stable" windows.  The fit isn't
resolving two timescales -- it's using the second exponential purely as
extra curve-fitting flexibility, and reports whatever d1 falls out once d2
is pushed against its floor.  d1 only starts moving once the window is large
enough that d2 escapes its bound (from ~1.0-1.2 MGy on), at which point d1
is exactly as window-dependent as it was as a single exponential (see the
table above).  There is no window in which "the fast part" is both fitted to
enough points to be a real fit AND free of this degeneracy.

A FOURTH attempt, also tried in response to a direct request: fit a straight
line instead of an exponential, on the reasoning that a curve does not need a
half-life at all.  Made no difference -- the same instability shows up in the
FITTED SLOPE.  Restricting a linear fit to increasing windows (0.1, 0.3, 0.5,
1.0, 2.0 MGy) at DTNB_25 gives slope -0.062, -0.030, -0.022, -0.013, -0.009
per MGy -- a 7-fold spread, worse at 5 % T (15-fold) -- because the trace is
genuinely concave (fast fall, then shallower) and any straight line only ever
reports the average steepness of the stretch it was fitted to.  Fitting a
polynomial instead of a line does not fix this either, for the same reason
with the roles reversed: at a FIXED 1.0 MGy window, the fitted derivative at
dose = 0 goes from -0.013 (degree 1) to -0.119 (degree 10) as the polynomial
degree rises, with no sign of settling, even though every one of those fits
lies visually on top of the data (R2 > 0.98 from degree 3 up).  A
non-parametric local derivative (Savitzky-Golay) fails the same way from a
third direction, at 5 % and 25 % T where the raw data are dense (~960
frames): too small a smoothing window and the derivative is dominated by
frame-to-frame measurement noise (no plateau from 5 to 121 points), too
large a window and it blends in the later, shallower part of the curve,
reproducing the linear-fit problem.  At 50 % and 100 % T the derivative
LOOKS flat across small windows (5-21 points), but this is not a stable
local rate -- those conditions have only 1-2 raw frames within the dose
span those windows cover (131 and 30 raw frames total, against ~960 at
5/25 % T), so the interpolated curve is one straight line there and any
small window trivially returns its slope.  That is a sampling-density
artefact, not evidence that a local rate exists to be measured.

The common cause, stated plainly: "the initial rate" is not a single number
this data supports, under ANY of the four methods above, because it requires
choosing how much of a noisy, genuinely-curved trace counts as "initial" --
and every reasonable choice of that scale (fit window, model order, smoothing
window) gives a materially different answer.  This is not a shortcoming of
any one fitting method; a derivative at a point is inherently a statement
about a neighbourhood, and there is no data-determined size for that
neighbourhood here.

What the radiation-damage literature actually reports for this kind of
dose-response curve is not an instantaneous rate but a SATURATION dose -- a
D1/2 or D90, the dose at which the change has covered a fixed fraction of the
total change it is going to make -- and that quantity does not have the
problem above, because it is read off a single point on the curve rather than
averaged over an arbitrary neighbourhood.  This project already has exactly
that: quantify.fit_single_exponential fits A = A0 + B*exp(-D/d1) to each
difference trace and reports D90 (defined against the measured range, not
extrapolated), with a decay_observed_frac diagnostic that correctly catches
non-identifiability rather than hiding it -- at 100 % T the fit only observes
14 % of its own modelled decay, and D90 there is correctly flagged as a lower
bound (>1.9 MGy) rather than reported as a bare number.  This is table 9 and
is already plotted in figure 2 panel C; it is the answer to "extract a rate"
that this project settled on before the four attempts above, and nothing in
this document supersedes it.

What is robust, and used throughout the REST of this document (the viability
figures and dose-budget table, which are about detectability rather than
about fitting the decay's shape): the detection dose, where a curve first
clears the measured noise floor.  It is a single crossing point, not an
average over a window, so it shares D90's immunity to this problem.

HOW TO REGENERATE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  PYTHONPATH=crystalspec/src python -m crystalspec.run \
      --config crystalspec/config/experiment.toml --out crystalspec/outputs/

  Figures V1-V3 and table_V1_viability.csv come from crystalspec/viability.py,
  which reads the dose budget the main pipeline computes and does not re-derive
  any quantity independently.
