% ---------------- METHODS ----------------

\subsubsection{Spectroscopy}

DTNB soaked R37S crystals were loaded into an EVAL film sandwich as shown in
\ref{fig:thinfilm} and described in \parencite{axford2016thinfilms,
JAHO2024fixedtarget}. The thin film sandwich was mounted on the beamline
goniometer using a tweezer pin; each region of the film was initially aligned
using the OAV so that it was coincident with the X-ray beam, and then rotated
45\(^{\circ}\) so that the crystal slurry was aligned with the UV-Vis
spectrometer optics. Spectra were recorded on an Andor SR303i spectrograph
fitted with a DU970P electron-multiplying CCD, using a 150 l mm\(^{-1}\)
grating blazed at 300 nm and a 100 \(\mu\)m entrance slit. Each spectrum
comprised five accumulations of 20 ms, and spectra were acquired as a
kinetics series with a cycle time of 0.1 s for 1000 cycles, giving 100 s of
continuous acquisition per film position. Because the probe beam samples a
region of the thin film rather than a single crystal, each measurement is an
ensemble over several randomly oriented microcrystals together with the
surrounding mother liquor; the random orientation is advantageous, as it
averages out the orientation dependent absorbance that complicates
single crystal in crystallo spectroscopy. Five film positions were recorded
for each condition. Cryocooled measurements were made on single crystals at
100 K, with single accumulated spectra recorded before irradiation and after
each successive X-ray exposure.

Absorbed X-ray dose was estimated using RADDOSE-3D \parencite{dickerson2024doses}.
The average whole crystal dose for a 0.1 s exposure at full beam was
0.104 MGy, giving a dose rate of 1.042 MGy s\(^{-1}\) at 100 \% transmission,
and dose was taken to accumulate linearly with elapsed time and to scale with
the nominal attenuator transmission. It should be noted that the RADDOSE-3D
model used a 50 \(\times\) 50 \(\mu\)m beam at 5 \(\times\)
10\(^{12}\) ph s\(^{-1}\), which differs from the beam parameters recorded for
the diffraction experiment; the absolute dose scale is therefore provisional,
although comparisons between transmission levels are unaffected.

Spectra were analysed using custom Python code (crystalspec), which reads the
raw Andor output directly and regenerates every figure and table in a single
pass. Three properties of the data determined the analysis. First, the
absorbance of a microcrystal thin film is dominated by scattering rather than
by absorption, and the scattering magnitude varied by a factor of two to four
between film positions within a single condition; absolute absorbances were
therefore never averaged across positions. Second, mother liquor is present in
the probe path, so absolute absorbance carries a solvent contribution that
cannot be separated from the crystalline one. Third, the crystalline volume
fraction within the beam is unknown and varies between positions, and
consequently no amplitude reported here has been converted to a concentration
or to an absolute TNB occupancy.

Each spectrum was therefore expressed as a difference against the mean of the
first five frames recorded at that same film position, before irradiation. A
scattering background of the form
\(A_{\mathrm{scatter}}(\lambda) = c(\lambda/500)^{-n} + b\) was then fitted
over 470--725 nm, a region in which neither TNB, DTNB nor the protein absorbs,
and subtracted across the full spectral range. Held out cross validation, in
which the background was fitted over 500--725 nm and its prediction tested
against the withheld 470--500 nm region, showed that a power law described the
scattering better than either a linear or a constant background (median
residuals of 0.0056 and 0.0060 against 0.0122 absorbance units respectively),
but distinguished only weakly between exponents. The exponent was accordingly
fixed at \(n = 3\); allowing it to float produced values that ran to the
imposed bounds for a fifth of the DTNB spectra and a third of the apo spectra.
The residual sensitivity to this choice is reported as a systematic
uncertainty rather than concealed in a point estimate.

The detector baseline was reset periodically during acquisition to counteract
upward drift, which appears in the data as a step displacing the whole
spectrum between consecutive frames. Such steps were detected as changes in
the mean absorbance over 500--650 nm exceeding 0.01 absorbance units between
successive frames, a threshold approximately thirty times the frame to frame
noise of 3.2 \(\times\) 10\(^{-4}\) determined from the conditions in which no
resets occurred. Isolated resets were removed by subtracting the wavelength
resolved offset across the step from all subsequent frames. Where resets
arrived in bursts, defined as three or more within 2 s, the trace was no
longer treated as a continuous measurement of a single sample state and the
analysis window was ended at the onset of the burst. Reset counts, stitching
and truncation points are given for every film position in Table
\ref{tab:qc_positions} and illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:spec_qc}.

The TNB absorbance was quantified by integrating the scatter corrected
difference spectra over 395--430 nm and dividing by the window width, giving a
mean absorbance change. A fixed window was used in preference to peak fitting
because the 412 nm TNB maximum reported in solution is not resolved in
crystallo; it appears only as a shoulder on a substantially larger feature
near 330--345 nm, and a free Gaussian fitted to it returns a centre that
wanders across the full permitted range together with an amplitude that
collapses to zero for a large fraction of timepoints. Two further windows were
integrated in parallel: 300--325 nm, which gains absorbance under irradiation,
and 470--500 nm, which should remain at zero after scatter correction and
therefore serves as an internal null. Uncertainties were obtained by
percentile bootstrap over the five film positions, resampling the unit of
replication rather than assuming normality, and significance was assessed by
exact permutation over all 252 possible five against five splits.


% ---------------- RESULTS ----------------

\subsubsection{Spectroscopy}

The initial spectra of DTNB soaked crystals show a broad absorbance maximum
near 330--345 nm with a shoulder extending through 412 nm, the reported
maximum for TNB in solution. Contributions are expected from TNB, from
unreacted DTNB near 330 nm, and from the protein itself below 300 nm,
including the disulphide absorbance near 260 nm
\parencite{faraj2019cysreactivity}. The 412 nm band is not resolved from the
larger feature at shorter wavelength, and the analysis was conducted in
difference space accordingly.

Referencing each film position to its own pre-irradiation state reveals a
reproducible response to irradiation (Figure \ref{fig:dtnb_signature}). In
both DTNB soaked and unlabelled crystals a positive feature grows near 310 nm
as dose accumulates, consistent with a general radiation driven process that
is independent of the label. In the DTNB soaked crystals, and not in the
matched controls, this is accompanied by a negative feature spanning
approximately 370--450 nm and overlapping the TNB band. Integrated over
395--430 nm, the difference signal at the highest dose analysed reaches
\(-0.011\) at 5 \% transmission, \(-0.014\) at 25 \%, \(-0.006\) at 50 \% and
\(-0.025\) at 100 \%, against values of \(+0.006\) to \(+0.031\) in the
corresponding unlabelled controls (Table \ref{tab:spec_summary}).

The sign of this signal separates the two soak states cleanly at the three
lower dose rates. Comparing the dose integrated 395--430 nm signal between
DTNB soaked and matched unlabelled films, all five DTNB positions fall below
all five control positions at 5 \%, 25 \% and 50 \% transmission, giving the
smallest attainable exact permutation probability for a five against five
comparison (\(p = 0.0079\), \(p = 0.032\) after Bonferroni correction for four
comparisons) with effect sizes of \(d = -1.8\), \(-2.3\) and \(-2.3\)
respectively. At 100 \% transmission the separation is lost
(\(p = 0.18\), \(d = -1.0\)): the unlabelled films themselves develop a
negative feature in this region at the highest dose rate, and the labelled and
unlabelled distributions overlap. This condition also retains the least data,
since baseline instability truncated all five positions early, and the loss of
significance may reflect reduced power rather than a genuine absence of the
effect. The negative feature should therefore be regarded as label specific at
the three lower dose rates, and as not separable from the general radiation
response at the highest.

The loss of TNB absorbance is dose dependent (Figure \ref{fig:dose_dependence}).
A stretched exponential in dose described the 395--430 nm signal better than
linear, single exponential or biexponential alternatives for every DTNB
condition, by both information criterion and held out error, improving on the
linear null by 75 to 252 AIC units. The characteristic dose recovered from
this fit rises with transmission, from 0.25 MGy at 5 \% through 1.09 MGy at
25 \% to 3.6--9.2 MGy at the two highest settings; an independent estimate
obtained by singular value decomposition of the same difference matrices gives
0.34, 1.50, 3.44 and 4.89 MGy across the same series. The two approaches
differ in detail, and the bootstrap intervals on the band integration route
are wide, spanning more than an order of magnitude at 5 \% and 25 \%
transmission, but both indicate that a larger absorbed dose is required to
produce the same loss of signal when that dose is delivered more rapidly.
Such an inverse dose rate dependence is characteristic of a process limited by
the diffusion or recombination of radiolytic species rather than by the
absorbed dose alone. It should be noted that the shape parameter of the
stretched exponential reached its imposed upper bound in all four DTNB
conditions, so the characteristic doses derived from these fits are
approximate and should not be quoted as converged estimates.

The response at 100 K is markedly attenuated (Figure \ref{fig:rt_vs_cryo}). In
the single DTNB soaked crystal measured at 100 K the integrated 395--430 nm
signal remains at \(+0.002\) through two successive X-ray exposures and rises
only to \(+0.005\) after the third, in contrast to the negative excursion of
\(-0.011\) to \(-0.025\) reached at room temperature. Only at the final,
unattenuated exposure does the cryocooled crystal depart appreciably from its
starting state. The comparison is necessarily qualitative, since the
cryocooled measurements were made on single crystals with an exposure index
rather than a calibrated dose axis, and only one DTNB soaked crystal was
measured; nevertheless the direction is consistent with the expected
suppression of secondary radiation chemistry at cryogenic temperature.

Taken together these observations indicate that the TNB moiety is lost from
the crystal under X-ray irradiation at room temperature, at a rate that is
slow enough to permit measurements on the order of seconds but fast enough to
constrain the useful lifetime of the label in a serial experiment. At the
lowest dose rate examined, half of the total change occurs within
approximately 0.25 MGy, which is well below the dose accumulated in a typical
room temperature serial data collection. The rate is, however, many orders of
magnitude slower than the ultraviolet driven disulphide cleavage that this
probe is intended to report, which has been observed on femtosecond to
picosecond timescales in L-cysteine dimers
\parencite{ochmann2024uv_Lcys_XAS}; the radiation induced loss of TNB is
therefore a background process to be accounted for rather than a competing
signal on the timescale of an ultrafast pump probe measurement.

A third quantification approach, decomposition of the difference spectra into
reference profiles for TNB, DTNB and the protein, was attempted as an
independent check and proved inconclusive for a structural reason. Over the
fitted region the DTNB depletion profile and the empirical protein growth
profile are anti correlated with a Pearson coefficient of \(-0.97\), so that
a reduction in one can be traded against an increase in the other at almost no
cost in residual. Band positions and widths are consequently not identifiable
from these data, and were held at the literature values of 412 nm for TNB and
325 nm for DTNB rather than reported as measured in crystallo shifts. Within
that constraint, inclusion of the TNB and DTNB profiles improved the Bayesian
information criterion only at 50 and 100 \% transmission, the protein and
background model being preferred at the two lower dose rates. The residuals of
all fits concentrate between 286 and 300 nm, at the blue edge of the measured
range where the reference set contains no component; this is a genuine
limitation of describing a crystalline spectrum with solution reference
profiles rather than a failure of the fitting procedure.

A caveat applies to all of the above. The internal null window at
470--500 nm, which should remain at zero once the scattering background has
been removed, instead drifts to between 12 and 29 \% of the co-located TNB
signal at the highest doses in the DTNB conditions. Part of the apparent
partial recovery of the signal at high dose coincides with the region in which
this drift is largest, and may therefore reflect incomplete scatter correction
rather than genuine photochemistry. The magnitude of the TNB signal is
additionally sensitive to the assumed scattering exponent, shifting by 11 to
60 \% depending on condition when the exponent is varied between 1 and 4.
These systematic effects bound the precision of the values reported here,
although they are considerably smaller than the difference between the
labelled and unlabelled films at the three lower dose rates, and do not affect
the sign of that difference.
