The measurements reported here were made on thin films carrying many randomly
oriented microcrystals in mother liquor, and the label-specific signal was
recovered only as a difference between DTNB-soaked and matched apo film sets.
It is worth setting out what that implies for a joint experiment in which
diffraction and absorbance are recorded from the same sample, because the
constraints are quantitative and they do not all point the same way.

Three separate questions have to be answered, and they have different answers.

The first is whether the label survives a diffraction dataset. It does. The
label-specific absorbance in the 408-416 nm window falls at 7.5 per cent of the
label present per MGy at the lowest dose rate examined (0.26 MGy per second),
6.8 per cent per MGy at 0.52 MGy per second and 3.0 per cent per MGy at 1.04
MGy per second. Room-temperature crystals of unrelated proteins have reported
lifetimes between 0.10 and 0.37 MGy (Owen et al., 2012), so a complete
room-temperature dataset costs between 0.3 and 2.8 per cent of the label. On
this evidence the spectroscopic probe is not the limiting factor; the crystal
is. A labelled structure determined at room temperature is a structure of the
labelled protein, not of a partially stripped one.

The second question is whether the change can be watched on the crystal being
collected from, and here the answer is no, or at best marginally. The
label-specific change reaches the measurement noise floor of approximately
0.004 absorbance units after 0.25 MGy at 25 per cent transmission, 0.53 MGy at
50 per cent and 0.70 MGy at 100 per cent. Only the first of these is comparable to the
lifetime reported at a matching collection speed (0.257 MGy for continuous
collection below 11 Hz), and it sits essentially at that limit rather than
inside it. In
wall-clock terms the detection times are 0.95, 1.01 and 0.68 seconds against
single-crystal lifetimes of 0.37-1.43, 0.19-0.72 and 0.09-0.36 seconds
respectively. A single room-temperature crystal is destroyed before its label
signal becomes measurable.

The third question is whether collecting faster resolves this, and the answer
is that it makes matters worse. Faster collection extends crystal lifetime
through the inverse dose-rate effect, and that effect is real: the lifetimes
quoted above rise from 0.186 MGy for stop-start collection to 0.257 MGy for
continuous collection below 11 Hz and 0.373 MGy at 25 Hz. But the label loss
per unit dose falls faster than the lifetime rises. Expressed as the ratio of
detection dose to the lifetime reported at a comparable collection speed, the
figure of merit is 0.97 at 0.26 MGy per second, 1.76 at 0.52 and 1.89 at 1.04.
Each ratio uses the lifetime measured at its own collection speed rather than a
single bound, since the inverse dose-rate effect makes the lifetime itself a
function of speed; comparing against one fixed lifetime would conceal the
trade-off. Speed is therefore not the lever. The
same suppression of damage per unit dose that protects the crystal also
suppresses the signal the experiment is trying to record, and the two effects
very nearly cancel.

What does work is signal averaging. Detection dose scales as the noise floor
divided by the loss rate, and the noise floor of an averaged measurement falls
as the square root of the number of contributing crystals. Averaging four
crystals brings the detection dose to 0.125 MGy, ten crystals to 0.079 MGy and
twenty-five to 0.050 MGy, all comfortably inside a single-crystal lifetime.
This is the reason the present measurements were made on films rather than
single crystals, and it should be read as the necessary design rather than a
compromise: the required sensitivity is only reachable by accumulating
absorbance across many crystals.

Two practical recommendations follow. First, for the spectroscopic arm the beam
should be attenuated rather than run at full transmission, since the lower dose
rate gives a two-and-a-half-fold larger signal per unit dose. This is the
opposite of the choice that maximises diffraction lifetime, and a joint
experiment must decide which arm to favour. Second, a serial approach in which
absorbance is accumulated across many small crystals while diffraction is
collected from each is better matched to the sensitivity available than an
attempt to follow the change on one large crystal.

It is worth being precise about the source of the position-to-position variation
in these measurements, because it is not pathlength. The thin-film sandwich
geometry fixes the gap: spacers of defined thickness set the separation between
the two polymer films, and published constructions of this type use spacers of
10 to 100 micrometres, giving total sandwich thicknesses between 36 and 126
micrometres (Axford et al., 2016). The
optical path through the sample is therefore approximately constant across
positions and between the DTNB and apo film sets. What varies is the material
occupying that path: the number, size and orientation of microcrystals in the
probe footprint, and the proportion of the path filled by mother liquor rather
than crystal. The observed 1.7 to 2.6-fold spread in ground-state label
absorbance between positions within a single condition is therefore a variable
crystalline volume fraction in a fixed path, not a variable path. The
consequence for the analysis is the same in either case, since neither can be
measured independently here, but the distinction matters for how the experiment
would be improved: a defined path is already available, and what a large single
crystal adds is a known and uniform absorber filling it. The alternative route, using
large single cryo-cooled crystals with a conventional online
microspectrophotometer, buys the pathlength and the sample stability needed for
absolute measurements but forfeits the room-temperature chemistry that motivated
the experiment.

CAVEATS

The diffraction lifetimes used throughout are literature values for three
unrelated proteins and were not measured on these crystals. Resolution against
dose from the same chips would replace this bracket with a directly comparable
number, and until that is available the comparison should be read as
order-of-magnitude.

The noise floor of 0.004 absorbance units is taken from the internal-zero
window of these film measurements and includes the mismatch between DTNB and
apo film sets. A single-crystal measurement in a sealed mount would plausibly
do better, in which case the detection doses quoted here are pessimistic.

The averaging argument assumes independent noise between crystals. Systematic
differences in crystal size, orientation and mother-liquor pathlength are not
independent and will set a floor that no amount of averaging removes; the
zero-dose offsets observed between matched film sets here, between 0.002 and
0.008 absorbance units, are a direct measure of that floor in the present
geometry, and they arise from differing crystalline content rather than differing
path.

Finally, the claim that the path is fixed rests on the published geometry of
thin-film sandwich platforms rather than on a measurement made on these chips.
The absorbance data cannot separate a variable path from a variable crystalline
volume fraction: at 600-700 nm, where no chromophore absorbs, the
position-to-position spread correlates with the label-band spread at r = 0.53 to
0.99, which is what either explanation predicts. The geometric argument, not this
correlation, is the reason for preferring the fixed-path interpretation.
